Four things that look alike, and are not

Client Logs, Attention, Notices and Pod Notes all show up as lists of short lines about a client. That is the only thing they have in common. This sheet explains each one in plain English, what makes it different from the other three, and why it earns its place.

The one-line difference

FeatureAnswers the questionWho it is forEnds when
Client Logs What happened? Anyone on the pod Never — it is history
Attention What do we owe? Whoever picks it up Somebody closes it with a reason
Notices What was I told? One named person That person reads or acknowledges it
Pod Notes What should the next specialist know? The pod, privately Never — it is a handover

The same event through all four

Meena has not logged a meal for three days. The 08:00 sweep runs and:

  • Client Log gets a line: “No meals logged, 3 days.” It is now part of her history whether or not anybody acts.
  • Attention raises one ticket: “Meena — three days without a meal log.” It sits on the board until a human closes it.
  • Notice is sent to her nutrition coach, by name: “A client you carry needs a look.” Only that coach sees it.
  • Pod Note gets nothing. A machine does not write handover notes — a coach does, after they have spoken to her.

Four different rows, four different lifetimes, four different readers. Collapsing any two of them loses something a person needs.

1 · Client Logs HISTORY

In one sentence

The client’s diary, written by everybody and edited by nobody.

What it actually is

Every record has a Logs tab. It is a single time-ordered list of everything that has happened to that client — meals and moods they logged themselves, sessions the team ticked off, plan changes, medical uploads, and the console’s own acts. Newest first, grouped by day.

It is merged, not copied. The log does not have its own table full of duplicated rows. The server reads the ten places the truth already lives, joins them with the handful of rows that only exist as log entries, and hands back one list. So a log line can never disagree with the thing it describes — there is only one copy.

What you can do with it

  • Filter by All / Client / Team / Plan / Medical. The count on each chip is the real total, worked out on the server, so it is right before you press it.
  • Set a date window — both ends included, so “to 4 Sept” really does include the 4th.
  • Press Show older to walk back through a record that may run to thousands of rows.
Behind the scenes: GET /clients/:id/logs takes the filter, the window and a cursor. It reads eleven sources, merges them newest-first, counts every bucket across the whole window, then returns one page. The cursor names a row, not a position — so new entries arriving at the top while you read cannot make page two repeat or skip anything.

Why it is useful

It is the answer to “what on earth happened here?” — asked by a coach picking up a client at short notice, or by anybody reviewing a decision months later. Because nothing in it can be edited or deleted, it is also the only one of the four you can trust as a record.

How it differs from the other three

vs Attention
A log says a thing happened. A ticket says somebody still owes work about it. The log never closes; the ticket exists in order to be closed.
vs Notices
The log is for everyone who can open the record. A notice is addressed to one person and appears on nobody else’s screen.
vs Pod Notes
The log records what happened. A pod note records what somebody thinks — an opinion, a warning, a bit of context.

2 · Attention WORK

In one sentence

The list of things somebody still owes, with a name against each one.

What it actually is

Home → Attention. Each row is a ticket about one client: a title, how loud it is (Info, Watch, High, Critical), the evidence behind it, and who is holding it. Tickets are raised by the 08:00 sweep or by a colleague, and they stay on the board until a human closes one.

The same tab also shows this morning’s reading underneath — one line per client, rebuilt every night. That half is a summary and disappears tomorrow. The tickets above it do not.

What you can do with it

  • Acknowledge — “I have seen this.”
  • Pick up — “I am working it now.”
  • Take it / Hand back / Assign — put it on your own list, return it to the pod, or (if your seat allows) put it on somebody else’s.
  • Resolve or Dismiss — both require you to type a reason. Resolve means it was dealt with; Dismiss means it was never a problem. They are different words on purpose.
Behind the scenes: every ticket carries a dedupeKey with a uniqueness rule enforced by the database, not by code. So the sweep can run twice, or two sweeps can run at once, and Meena still gets one ticket rather than three. When a ticket is closed its key is retired, which means if the same problem comes back next week it correctly raises a new ticket instead of silently re-opening the old one.
Why the reason is compulsory: a resolved ticket with no explanation is a ticket nobody can audit. The console asks for it before sending, and the server refuses the close without it — so it cannot be skipped by going round the console.

Why it is useful

It turns “someone should probably look at that” into a named, dated, closeable piece of work. It is the only one of the four where accountability is the point.

How it differs from the other three

vs Client Logs
A ticket is a job with an owner and an ending. A log line is a fact with neither.
vs Notices
A ticket is work anybody on the pod can pick up. A notice is a message to one person. A sweep usually writes both: the ticket is the job, the notice is the tap on the shoulder telling you the job exists.
vs Pod Notes
A ticket asks you to do something. A pod note asks nothing — it just tells you something.

3 · Notices MESSAGES

In one sentence

Your own inbox — things the system said to you, by name.

What it actually is

Home → Notices. Escalations, SLA warnings, session reminders, leave decisions and celebrations. Every other list in the console is filtered to the clients you carry; this one is filtered to you. Two people looking at this tab at the same desk see different rows.

Each notice has three states, and they mean different things:

  • Unread — it arrived and you have not opened it.
  • Read — you opened it. This happens by itself.
  • Acknowledged — you deliberately said “I have this.” It is stamped with the time.

What you can do with it

  • Filter to Unread only, by kind, or by severity.
  • Open one — it marks itself read and takes you to the client it is about.
  • Acknowledge it, when the sender needs to know it landed.
  • Show older to page back through the lot.
Behind the scenes: GET /notices takes the recipient from your login token — never from the request. There is deliberately no way to ask for somebody else’s notices, because a filter that could name another person would be a privacy hole wearing a query parameter. Nothing in the console can send a notice either; only server-side sweeps raise them, so nobody can post a message in the system’s name.
Why scrolling past does not mark them read: other tabs clear their badge once you have looked at the board. This one does not. “Acknowledged” is a claim somebody may be chased on, so it has to mean a person decided — not that a row was on screen for a moment.

Why it is useful

Without it, everything the system wants to tell you competes with everything it wants to tell the other nine seats. This is the one list where nothing on it is somebody else’s problem.

How it differs from the other three

vs Attention
Closest relative, and still not the same. A notice tells one person; a ticket is work the pod shares. You acknowledge a notice in a second; you close a ticket after doing something.
vs Client Logs
A notice is addressed to you and can be about no client at all (your leave was approved). A log line belongs to a client and is read by anybody who opens them.
vs Pod Notes
A notice is written by the system to a person. A pod note is written by a person to the pod.

4 · Pod Notes PRIVATE

In one sentence

What one specialist leaves for the next — and the client never sees it.

What it actually is

A panel on the client record’s Overview, straight under the care team. Short notes written by the coaches on that client, for the coaches on that client. The handover sentence: “She got tearful when we reached the sleep questions — go gently there.”

This is the one thing on the record the client must never read. That is not managed by hiding it in the app. The server refuses the client role outright, before it even works out whose record this is, and no client-facing route returns these rows at any point. There is an automated test that opens every endpoint the mobile app can reach and fails the build if a pod note appears in any of them.

What you can do with it

  • Write a note — up to 4,000 characters, the same ceiling a care-circle message has.
  • Edit your own. The panel then says “edited”, and means it — the stamp is only set by a human edit, never by a background job touching the row.
  • Remove your own. This takes it off the panel; the record keeps it. Somebody removing a line cannot remove the reason a decision was made.
  • A seat that manages people can correct anybody’s note. Everyone else gets a refusal — and the refusal itself is written to the audit trail.

Why it is useful

Real care handovers are full of things that are true, useful, and wrong to say to the client’s face. Without a private lane those things either get written where the client can see them, or they do not get written at all — and the next coach starts from nothing.

How it differs from the other three

vs the Care Circle
The circle is the room the client is in. Pod notes are the corridor outside it.
vs Client Logs
The log is what happened. A pod note is what a person made of it.
vs Attention
A note asks nothing of anybody. Nobody is assigned it, and it never closes.
vs Notices
A notice goes to one named person. A pod note is left for whoever opens the record next — including someone who has not joined yet.

Put simply

Client Log“She missed three days of meals.” — a fact
Attention“Somebody call her about it.” — a job
Notice“Priya, this one is yours.” — a message
Pod Note“Money is tight at home right now — go gently.” — a confidence
